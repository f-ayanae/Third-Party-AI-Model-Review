Third-Party Model Review · Public-evidence readiness review · 12 November 2026

Should Mainkai Direktbank adopt the bureau's 12-criterion score (v1.0) as its primary bureau score for consumer instalment loans?

Prepared by Fiona Edocket as a portfolio case study. Mainkai Direktbank AG is fictional; a large German credit bureau is real and anonymised in this version. This is a public-evidence readiness review and method demonstration. It is not an audit, a validation, a certification or a legal opinion, and it uses public sources only.
Recommendation: Defer pending evidence
Applied mechanically under outcome Rule 3 of the scope: a core element, outcomes analysis, cannot be rated on public evidence. Findings: 0 Critical, 9 High, 4 Medium, 1 Low.

Why not yet

  1. No outcomes evidence for Mainkai's applicants. The only public performance figure is a company-reported Gini of 0.6, with no sample, period or product. That figure covers the bureau's whole population and cannot be compared with a Gini on Mainkai's applicants. Mainkai's test is relative: on the same retro-scored applicants, the new score may not lose more than 3 Gini points against the current score, and must be calibrated by pricing band. Only a retrospective scoring of Mainkai's past applicants can show this (F02).
  2. A legal question opens on 20 November 2026. New §37a(2) BDSG bars address data from scores, and the Bundestag committee calls their use impermissible without exception. Criterion 4, age of current address, carries up to 94 points. The bureau has not publicly explained how it treats this criterion, and §37a conditions the lender's use as well as the score's creation (F01).
  3. Thin-file applicants hit a ceiling. In the calculator, a person one year into the German credit file cannot score above 743 even with a perfect record. That is Class 2. Reaching Class 1 takes between 2 and 5 years, depending on annual bands that are not published. No public data shows whether this matches observed risk for under-25s or recent arrivals (F03).

What is in good shape

  • All twelve criteria and their maximum points are public. The calculator reproduces every published value, and points move in the expected direction in every criterion.
  • Each bureau report shows points per criterion, so a person-level explanation of a decline is feasible today.
  • The bureau reports an external methodological review by Fraunhofer IAIS and an acceptance by the competent data protection authority. The reports themselves are not public (F05).

What would change the answer

If the bureau supplies E01, E02, E03 and E07 by 31 January 2027, Model Validation can rate outcomes analysis. If no Critical finding emerges, the outcome rules then allow approve with conditions, with each High finding closed by a named owner before go-live (assumed 1 April 2027).

1. What was reviewed and how

Decision under review. Mainkai Direktbank AG is a fictional Frankfurt direct bank supervised by BaFin, with about 450,000 retail customers. It wants to use the bureau's new 12-criterion score as its primary bureau score for the Ratenkredit, an unsecured instalment loan of EUR 1,000 to 50,000 over 12 to 96 months. The score would set an auto-decline cut-off, one of five pricing bands and the maximum loan amount, across about 120,000 applications a year. Applicants in the middle band, about 20%, go to an underwriter with override authority. Mainkai's cut-offs are calibrated to the older bank-sector score, which the bureau is phasing out.

Roles. The bureau is the provider and Mainkai the deployer under the AI Act, if the score is an AI system (section 6). Under GDPR, the bureau is an independent controller and Mainkai the controller for the credit decision. Applicants are data subjects and, under the AI Act, affected persons. The role trap: if Mainkai blended the score with its own data into a new model, it would become a provider. The scope uses the score as delivered, and cut-offs are policy rules.

Method. The review has four strands. (1) A source register: 49 sources, each recorded as a text excerpt with a checksum (the two statutes, MaRisk and the act in BGBl. 2026 I Nr. 139, are also saved as original PDFs with SHA-256 hashes), tagged by tier, and linked to 30 claims, each labelled fact or assessment. (2) Lab A: a calculator built from the published points table and tested with unit tests (tests/test_calculator.py). (3) Lab B: an outcomes harness, run on 303,949 Bondora loans as proxy evidence about a different lender and model. (4) A desk review against SR 26-2 and PRA SS1/23 elements A to L, with EBA GL/2020/06 section 4.3.4, MaRisk (Circular 06/2026), DORA, the AI Act as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, §37a BDSG, §505a BGB and GDPR Articles 15 and 22.

Evidence standard. Each item is rated publicly available and adequate, available but inadequate, not publicly available, not yet due or not applicable. Absence from the public record is not a failing: the bureau's evidence duties run to its lending customers under contract, so every gap is framed as something Mainkai should request. Calculator results describe the published table, not how the bureau scores real people.

Ratings. Critical means Mainkai could not lawfully use the score, or could not meet a deployer or §37a duty, and no fix is workable before go-live. High means a material gap that a contract condition or pre-go-live evidence could close. Medium means a gap to close within 6 months of go-live, and Low a good-practice improvement.

Out of scope: independent validation, any audit or certification opinion, legal advice, Mainkai's affordability model, the bureau's fraud and identity products, conformity assessment, IRB use and a full ISO/IEC 42001 assessment. The author is not independent of the work and had no access to the bureau's data, code or contracts.

2. The artefact: a calculator built from the published points table

The bureau publishes each criterion and the points it can earn. The calculator (lab/calculator.py) adds the twelve criteria together. There is no base score. The maxima sum to 999, and the floor of 100 comes from the payment criterion, which never falls below 100 while a score exists. An earlier draft of the plan added a 100-point base, which would have produced a range of 200 to 1,099. A unit test now prevents that error from returning.

Figure 3. Calculator scores for six constructed profiles (published values, step-hold). Each profile is a documented case in lab/run_lab.py, not a sample of real people.

Reading the results. The table rewards time in the file, and it does so by design: the bureau says points reflect the historical default rates of each group. A lower score for a short history is therefore not a defect in itself. It becomes a problem only if observed default rates for those groups do not support it, and that evidence is not public (F03). Two design features stand out. First, entry dips: holding no credit card earns 24 points, while a new card earns 0 to 23 in its first year, so opening a first product lowers the score for a while. Second, the established renter who moved six months ago (A2) still reaches Class 1 because her long card and contract history carries her; the address criterion mainly separates people who have stayed put for years from everyone else.

Figure 4. One event at a time, applied to the under-25 profile A3 (score 719). Taking Mainkai's own 60-month loan costs 78 points across three criteria (loans taken, remaining term, credit status) and drops the profile to Class 4 (F13).

What this means for the decision

  • Compare scores on the same applicants, not levels. An absolute Gini is not comparable across populations, and this sample holds approved loans only. The decisive test is paired: new against current score on the same retro-scored applicants, with a bootstrap interval for the difference (E02), for both the points score and any bands Mainkai will use.
  • A rising average is not enough. Rank-ordering held across most of the scale but broke at the risky end, which is exactly where Mainkai's auto-decline cut-off will sit. Default rates per score band with intervals, per vintage, belong in E02.
  • The same score can mean different risk in different groups. On this data observed defaults reached 2.34 times the expected level in one segment and 0.72 in another. Mainkai cannot see this for under-25s or recent arrivals without E04, and the thin-file ceiling in section 2 makes those the groups to test first.
  • Monitor by band, not just in aggregate. A near-flat portfolio default rate hid worsening in seven of eight grades. Mainkai's monitoring pack (E10) needs band-level observed-to-expected tracking.

F01 · Address-age criterion and the s.37a BDSG address-data exclusionfact and assessment Not publicly available High
Observation
From 20 Nov 2026, s.37a(2) No.1(d) BDSG allows a probability value to be created or used only if address data are not used; the committee report calls their use impermissible without exception (L01, L02). The old s.31(1) No.3 restricted only the exclusive use of address data; s.37a bans their use outright. Criterion 4, age of current address, carries up to 94 points. Two readings are open: a broad one, under which residence time is derived from address history and so falls under the ban, and a narrow one, under which the ban targets geographic location (geoscoring) and residence time is a stability measure. Commentators say no court has decided between them, and the bureau has not publicly explained its treatment; its 11 Nov 2026 announcement, as reported, deals with older scores and does not mention this criterion (L03, L21, V17).
Why it matters
Section 37a conditions use as well as creation, so Mainkai is directly exposed. If the criterion is changed or removed, every score shifts and cut-offs set on today's table are stale.
Condition
The bureau to give a written legal position on criterion 4 and confirm the exact model version Mainkai will receive after 20 Nov 2026. If the criterion changes, the bureau supplies the revised table and a score-migration analysis before cut-offs are set.

F02 · No outcomes evidence on Mainkai's own applicantsfact Not publicly available High
Observation
The only public performance figure is a company-reported Gini of 0.6 with no sample, period, product or interval (V12). That figure is measured on the bureau's whole population and cannot be compared with a Gini on Mainkai's applicants, which is lower by construction. Mainkai's tolerance is therefore relative: on the same retro-scored applicants, the new score may not lose more than 3 Gini points against the current score (lower 95% bound of the paired difference), and must be calibrated within tolerance in every pricing band. Testing a new score version on a bank's own book needs the bureau to score the bank's historical applicants retrospectively (V20). Why it matters
Outcomes analysis is a core element and cannot be rated on public evidence. Under outcome rule 3 this alone defers the decision. MaRisk requires Mainkai to assess a third-party model's suitability before use and validate it regularly (AT 4.3.4 Tz. 2 and 5), and to form its own judgement of default risk rather than rely exclusively on external credit assessments (BTO 1.2 Tz. 5) (L11).

The bureau to retro-score a sample of Mainkai Ratenkredit applications (24 to 36 months, approvals and declines) with both the new and the current score. Mainkai Model Validation computes the paired Gini difference with a bootstrap interval, calibration by pricing band and class migration.

F03 · Thin-file segments: calibration within segments not evidencedassessment Not publicly available High
Observation
No segment performance is public, so nothing shows that a given score carries the same default risk for thin-file applicants as for everyone else. A lower score for a short credit history is expected by design; the open question is whether the score is calibrated within those segments. Context from the calculator: time-dependent criteria hold 280 of 999 points, and with perfect payment behaviour and no mortgage a person one year into the German credit file reaches at most 743 (Class 2); Class 1 takes between 2 and 5 years, depending on unpublished annual bands. A consumer score app that displays the score says less active persons may score somewhat worse (V18); the consumer federation expects young people to be more affected (L14). On Bondora's data, the same grade carried between 0.72 and 2.34 times the expected default risk across segments (section 3, B03).
Why it matters
Mainkai names under-25s and residents under 3 years as thin-file segments. A lower score is acceptable if it reflects higher observed risk; without default rates by score band within each segment Mainkai cannot show that, apply its 0.80 approval-rate screen or write the FRIA.
Condition
The bureau to provide score distribution, default rate by score band and Gini within each age band and time-in-file band, and its bias examination. Mainkai computes approval-rate ratios on retro-scored data before setting cut-offs.

F10 · Decline notices, explanation and contest route must meet ss.30(6)-(7) and 37a(5) BDSGfact and assessment Available but inadequate High
Observation
Points per criterion allow a person-level explanation. From 20 Nov 2026, s.37a(5) gives applicants a right to contest and to a decision by a natural person against the lender (L04). The BGH held Art 15(1)(h) applies only where Art 22(1) is met, but a transmitted score followed by an adverse decision raises a presumption (L05). The act itself sets three lender duties for consumer loans (L01): under s.30(6), where the creditworthiness check is automated the borrower may demand human intervention, a clear explanation including the logic and risks, to state a view and a review, and must be told of these rights; under s.30(7) sentence 1, a rejection resulting from credit-agency information must be notified at once with the information received, details of the database consulted and the data categories considered; under s.30(7) sentence 2, a rejection resting on automated processing must also state the right to review by a person and the procedure to contest. Failing to inform under s.30(6) sentence 3 or s.30(7) is an administrative offence (s.43(1) No. 2 BDSG), and consumer associations can sue over ss.30 and 37a (s.2(2) No. 14 UKlaG as amended).
Why it matters
Auto-declines below the cut-off will usually meet the presumption and trigger both sentences of s.30(7), so every decline needs a compliant notice from the first day of go-live; a gap is a finable offence, not just a quality issue.
Condition
Mainkai to rebuild decline letters and the underwriter review script around the 12 criteria, name the bureau database and data categories in every decline that rests on bureau information (s.30(7) sentence 1), state the right to review by a person and the contest procedure in every automated decline (sentence 2), and agree with the bureau the data-correction turnaround for disputed entries.

6. AI Act: classification and readiness

Is it an AI system? A reasoned position

The case for. The points were learned from data by logistic regression, and the bureau describes the method as learning from examples and extracting statistical correlations (V12). The output is a prediction that shapes decisions about people, and Annex III point 5(b) names credit scoring of natural persons. The case against. Once fitted, the score is a fixed additive table. The Commission's guidelines on the definition place systems that improve or approximate well-established optimisation methods, naming linear and logistic regression, outside Article 3(1) (L09). Commentators dispute whether that carve-out reaches regression used to decide on people.

Position: treat the score as in scope, on a precautionary basis. The downside of wrongly assuming it is out of scope falls on Mainkai, as deployer, and on applicants. Most of the controls are required anyway by GDPR, §37a BDSG, §505a BGB and MaRisk. This position would change if the Commission, BaFin or the CJEU said that fixed regression scorecards fall outside Article 3(1).

  • Annex III point 5(b): applies. The fraud-detection exception is not engaged.
  • Article 6(3) derogation: unavailable. An Annex III system that profiles natural persons is always high-risk, and credit scoring is profiling under GDPR Article 4(4).
  • Timing and legacy rule: Annex III obligations apply from 2 December 2027 (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744). The score was placed on the market on 17 March 2026, so under Article 111(2) it is caught only if its design changes significantly after that date (L08). A change made before 2 December 2027, for instance to criterion 4 for §37a, would not by itself bring the score into scope. A re-weighting after that date could. Mainkai should plan for the obligations and not rely on the exemption.
  • Supervisor: since 29 July 2026, BaFin has been the AI Act market surveillance authority for creditworthiness systems of the institutions it supervises (KI-MIG, L10).

Deployer readiness (rows D1 to D10 of the obligation matrix)

Ten provider rows are expected from the bureau. Five are not publicly available, four are available but inadequate, and one is not yet due. Mainkai's own rows are mostly within its control. Two depend on the bureau: D2 (use in line with instructions) and D7 (DPIA). Both are blocked until the bureau provides instructions for use (P5). The DPIA is needed now under GDPR Article 35, whatever the AI Act position. The full matrix is in Annex B.

FRIA outline (Article 27, one page in the repo). Process: origination decline, pricing band and limit for the Ratenkredit. Frequency: continuous, about 120,000 applications a year. Affected groups: applicants aged 18 and over, with the thin-file groups named (under-25s, residents under 3 years, the self-employed). Specific risks: exclusion of short-history applicants, an address criterion under legal question, and data errors. Oversight: the refer band, override authority and the §37a(5) review route. Remedies: contest, data correction and complaints. Notification: to BaFin before first use. The FRIA needs E04 and E05 before it can be completed.

The 9th MaRisk amendment (Circular 06/2026 (BA), 30 June 2026) removes ICT services under Article 3(21) DORA from AT 9 (L11). Consequence. Model risk stays with Mainkai whatever the classification. SR 26-2 and EBA GL/2020/06 both hold the user responsible for understanding a vendor model.

§37a BDSG from 20 November 2026

Section 37a replaces §31 BDSG (BGBl. 2026 I Nr. 139). A probability value used to decide on a contract with a natural person may be created or used only if the following inputs are left out: special categories of data, age, gender, name, social-network data, account movements and address data. It also must not concern minors and must rest on a scientifically recognised method (L01, L04). On request, the creator must explain the data, the weighting of the most influential criteria and the meaning of the value (§37a(4)). The applicant can contest the decision and obtain a decision by a natural person from the lender (§37a(5)).

  • Address data (F01). Under the old §31, address data were allowed as long as they were not used exclusively. The new rule removes that allowance. Whether age of current address counts as address data is untested. The criterion measures duration rather than location, but it is derived from address records, and the bureau has said nothing public about it. This is the most time-critical item in the review.
  • Age. The score does not use the applicant's age. Time-based criteria correlate with age, but §37a bans age as an input, not correlated inputs. This is a fairness and bias question (F03), not a §37a breach.
  • Minors. The scope population is 18 and over, so this condition is met.

GDPR Articles 15 and 22, and the courts

Under CJEU C-634/21 (Scoring), 7 Dec 2023, a bureau's score can itself be an automated decision when a lender draws strongly on it. In BGH, judgments of 21 Oct 2026, I ZR 227/25 to 230/25 (claims against a credit bureau), the BGH set aside four appellate judgments against the bureau and remanded them. It held that the Article 15(1)(h) right to information about the logic exists only where all elements of Article 22(1) are met. It eased the burden of proof: if a score was transmitted and an adverse decision followed in time, decisiveness is presumed (L05). The bureau won those appeals; the rulings did not create a general new right. The broader right comes from §37a(4) from 20 November 2026. CJEU C-203/22 sets the content of an explanation: the procedure and principles actually applied, in a form the person can understand and use to contest (L07). For Mainkai, auto-declines below the cut-off will normally meet the presumption. Mainkai is also itself making an Article 22 decision, which needs a lawful basis under Article 22(2) and Article 22(3) safeguards.

§§505a ff. BGB (CCD2) from 20 November 2026

The creditworthiness test now requires that repayment be likely. Where the assessment uses automated processing, the consumer can request human intervention, an explanation and a review. §505b(2) and (3) BGB restrict data sources, excluding special categories and social-network data. The score is one input to Mainkai's test, not the test itself. Affordability remains out of scope.

12. Limitations

  • Public sources only. Sources were captured as text excerpts, and the hashes cover those excerpts. A byte-exact archive (Playwright, original PDFs) is an open QA item.
  • Criteria 5, 8 and 9, the first-year address value and the score-class boundaries were first taken from two agreeing secondary sources and then confirmed by the author on the bureau's own pages on 13 November 2026 (class boundaries V21; criterion values V22). The bureau's text for criterion 9 overlaps at exactly three years ('up to three years' 61, 'three years or more' 48); because it also says terms of up to three years have no negative impact, the calculator scores exactly 36 months as 61. The full band table is still requested (E06).
  • The calculator describes the published table. It cannot reproduce rounding, grace rules, debt-collection effects or data mapping.
  • Bondora results describe a different lender, model, population and default definition. The export has no PD, model version or age, so calibration, version comparison and age segments could not be tested on it.
  • The Bondora sample holds approved loans only. Applicants Bondora rejected are missing, so the riskiest part of the population is absent; this compresses the score range and lowers Gini whatever the score's design. The proxy's Gini level (0.37) is therefore not comparable with the bureau's reported 0.6, which is one reason Mainkai's tolerance is a paired comparison, and approval rates and reject inference cannot be tested on it.
  • The legal analysis is a reading by a non-lawyer, and the §37a address question is genuinely open.
  • Prepared without practitioner review. The findings, ratings, core-element choices and tolerances are the author's own judgement and have not been challenged by a second reviewer; the scenario test in section 8 shows how the outcome moves when the evidence changes.
